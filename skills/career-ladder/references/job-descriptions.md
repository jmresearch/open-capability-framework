# Per-level job descriptions — the hiring-facing projection of the ladder

The job descriptions are not a separate document that can drift from the ladder — they are the
ladder, projected level by level into the form a hiring panel uses. Every requirement in a JD must
be traceable to a matrix cell. A ladder you can't interview against is decoration.

## Format — one JD per level

```markdown
## <Level code> — <Title>

**Mission:** <one sentence: why this level exists>
**Scope:** <the blast radius at this level — task / component / capability / multiple teams / org / company>

### What you'll do
<6–10 present-tense responsibility bullets, drawn directly from this level's column of the matrix.
Same observable register as the cells — a candidate should recognize their actual day in these.>

### What we look for
<Competency requirements grouped by key area. Each bullet is a condensed form of a matrix cell at
this level — traceable, not invented. Do NOT add requirements that have no matrix cell.>

### Interview evidence guide
<For EACH key area:>
- **<Key area>** — *Ask:* <1–2 behavioral questions that probe it>
  - **At level:** <what an at-level answer sounds like — concrete signals>
  - **Below level:** <the tell that the candidate is one level down>
  - **Above level:** <the tell that the candidate exceeds this level>

### Level boundaries
- **You're at this level if:** <2–3 observable statements>
- **You're ready for the next level if:** <2–3 observable statements; omit for the top level>
```

## Rules

- **Traceability is the contract.** Every "what we look for" bullet condenses a specific matrix
  cell. If a requirement feels necessary but has no cell, the matrix is missing a competency — fix
  the matrix, then the JD.
- **No years-of-experience gates.** Prefer demonstrated behaviors. If years appear at all, label
  them explicitly as a guideline ("typically 5+ years, offered as a guideline — evidence of the
  behaviors below outweighs tenure").
- **The evidence guide is the load-bearing section.** Panels don't rate against cell prose in the
  room; they rate against "what does an at-level answer sound like." Below-level and above-level
  signals matter as much as at-level: they're how a panel avoids both over-leveling a polished
  storyteller and under-leveling a modest expert.
- **Behavioral questions, not trivia.** "Tell me about a time you caught a design problem in
  someone else's proposal" probes an observable; "explain CAP theorem" probes recall. Use the
  matrix cell's observable to generate the question.
- **Keep the terminal level honest.** For IC tracks, the terminal-level JD (typically Senior)
  should say it is a destination role, not a waypoint — the org hires people who may stay at this
  level indefinitely, and that is success.
- **Manager JDs follow the demonstrability rule.** Requirements for senior leadership levels are
  influence/leverage behaviors a candidate can evidence from their current seat — never "has
  managed managers for N years."

## How a hiring panel uses these

1. Pick the target level before the interview loop — the JD for that level is the rubric.
2. Assign key areas to interviewers so every area is probed by at least one person.
3. Each interviewer scores their assigned areas: below / at / above level, citing the candidate's
   actual statements as evidence.
4. In the debrief, disagreements resolve to the evidence guide's signals, not to gut feel.
5. A candidate strong in most areas but below-level in one or two is a leveling conversation, not
   an automatic rejection — the ladder makes the gap explicit and coachable.
