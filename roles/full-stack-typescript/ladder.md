# Full Stack TypeScript Engineer (IC Technical, E1-E6) - Canonical — Career Ladder

Canonical six-level IC ladder for engineers who own features end to end across UI, API, and data layers in a TypeScript-centered stack, consolidated by union from five independent blind generations; E3 Senior is the terminal level.


| Level | Title | Scope | Focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| E1 | Associate Full Stack Engineer | task | ships well-defined tasks across the stack with guidance and learns the stack |
| E2 | Full Stack Engineer | component | independently delivers complete features spanning UI |
| E3 | Senior Full Stack Engineer | capability / domain (terminal level) | owns a product capability across the stack and sets the local bar |
| E4 | Staff Full Stack Engineer | multiple teams | drives technical outcomes across multiple teams and multiplies other engineers |
| E5 | Senior Staff Full Stack Engineer | organization | sets technical direction and standards for the organization |
| E6 | Principal Full Stack Engineer | company | shapes company-level technical strategy and represents engineering externally |

## Frontend Engineering

#### UI Implementation

##### Component design & composition

*Anchor:* Meta, Thinking in React (React documentation, accessed 2026-07) — decomposing UI into a one-way data-flow component hierarchy is the canonical discipline for maintainable interfaces; component boundaries are where frontend complexity is either contained or leaks.

(Meta, Thinking in React (React documentation, accessed 2026-07); Larson, Staff Engineer: Leadership Beyond the Management Track (self-published, 2021))
- **E5 (= P5)** — Sets organization-wide component design strategy; anticipates how composition models should evolve. *Evidenced by:* Sets the organization's frontend architecture direction — design-system governance, framework and rendering-model choices — and removes the systemic obstacles blocking it. *Scope:* organization. *Why this level:* FE-01 P5 ('Sets organization-wide component design strategy; anticipates how composition models should evolve.') matches Senior Staff's org-wide authority over frontend architecture direction. (Meta, Thinking in React (React documentation, accessed 2026-07); Larson, Staff Engineer: Leadership Beyond the Management Track (self-published, 2021))
- **E6 (= P6)** — Defines what good component architecture means industrywide; shapes practice through widely adopted patterns. *Evidenced by:* Makes the company-level frontend bets (platform, framework generation, build-vs-buy for the design system) and is the named authority when they're contested. *Scope:* company. *Why this level:* FE-01 P6 ('Defines what good component architecture means industrywide; shapes practice through widely adopted patterns.') is the company/industry-scope bar — making the company-level frontend bets that are publicly contested and cited is that external-authority register. (Meta, Thinking in React (React documentation, accessed 2026-07); Larson, Staff Engineer: Leadership Beyond the Management Track (self-published, 2021))

#### State & Data Flow

*Anchor:* Facebook, Flux Application Architecture (2014) — unidirectional data flow and single-ownership of state are the reference discipline for predictable UIs, substituted for Redux's own docs (no bibliography entry) since Redux directly implements Flux and it is the catalog's own FE-05 P3/P4/P6 anchor; most frontend defects are state-synchronization defects.

##### Type-driven domain modeling

*Anchor:* Wlaschin, Domain Modeling Made Functional: Tackle Software Complexity with Domain-Driven Design and F# (Pragmatic Bookshelf, 2018) — making illegal states unrepresentable via types is the design discipline the type system exists to enforce; in a TypeScript-centered stack the type system is the cheapest place to delete whole defect classes before runtime.

(Larson, Staff Engineer: Leadership Beyond the Management Track (self-published, 2021))

##### Product thinking

*Anchor:* Cagan, INSPIRED: How to Create Tech Products Customers Love, 2nd Edition (Wiley, 2018) — engineers closest to the technology are a primary source of product insight when they engage with users and value, not just tickets; full stack engineers sit closest to the whole user experience of anyone in engineering.

##### Scoping, prioritization & estimation

*Anchor:* McConnell, Software Estimation: Demystifying the Black Art (Microsoft Press, 2006) — decomposition-based estimates as probabilistic forecasts, paired with Reinertsen's cost-of-delay economics for sequencing; trustworthy delivery is the currency senior ICs spend on everything else.

| OCF | E1 | E2 | E3 | E4 | E5 | E6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

- **E1 (= P1)** — Works highest-priority-first when told the priorities. *Evidenced by:* Breaks their tasks into day-sized pieces, flags slips the day they're known, and finishes what they start — working the agreed priority order rather than the interesting order. *Scope:* task. *Why this level:* PD-02 P1 ('Works highest-priority-first when told the priorities.') is the guided bar — breaking tasks into day-sized pieces and working the agreed priority order rather than the interesting order is following told priorities. (McConnell, Software Estimation: Demystifying the Black Art (Microsoft Press, 2006); Reinertsen, The Principles of Product Development Flow: Second Generation Lean Product Development (Celeritas, 2009); SFIA Foundation, Skills Framework for the Information Age, version 9 (2024))
- **E2 (= P2)** — Makes sound priority calls within scope; time-boxes uncertain work. *Evidenced by:* Slices features into thin vertical increments that ship value early — decomposing across frontend, backend, and migration work — estimates with stated assumptions, distinguishes must-have from nice-to-have, and says no to scope creep with a reason. *Scope:* component. *Why this level:* PD-02 P2 ('Makes sound priority calls within scope; time-boxes uncertain work.') matches slicing features into thin vertical increments with stated assumptions, distinguishing must-have from nice-to-have, within their own scope. (McConnell, Software Estimation: Demystifying the Black Art (Microsoft Press, 2006); Reinertsen, The Principles of Product Development Flow: Second Generation Lean Product Development (Celeritas, 2009); SFIA Foundation, Skills Framework for the Information Age, version 9 (2024))
- **E3 (= P3)** — Balances cost/value/risk across a domain; resists hype; decisions hold up over months. *Evidenced by:* Sequences a capability's work by expected impact, risk, and cost of delay — hardest unknowns first, states what they're deliberately not doing — and is trusted to trade quality, scope, and time inside the capability without escalation; their estimates are the ones planning trusts. *Scope:* capability / domain (terminal level). *Why this level:* PD-02 P3 ('Balances cost/value/risk across a domain; resists hype; decisions hold up over months.') is the capability-owning bar: sequencing a capability's work by impact, risk, and cost of delay, trusted without escalation, is balancing cost/value/risk across a domain verbatim. (McConnell, Software Estimation: Demystifying the Black Art (Microsoft Press, 2006); Reinertsen, The Principles of Product Development Flow: Second Generation Lean Product Development (Celeritas, 2009); SFIA Foundation, Skills Framework for the Information Age, version 9 (2024))
- **E4 (= P4)** — Expert at portfolio-level trade-offs; surfaces hidden assumptions; trusted tiebreaker. *Evidenced by:* De-risks and rebalances multi-team programs — finds the critical path across teams, keeps every team's slice independently shippable, and cancels or shrinks initiatives with sunk costs when the math says to. *Scope:* multiple teams. *Why this level:* PD-02 P4 ('Expert at portfolio-level trade-offs; surfaces hidden assumptions; trusted tiebreaker.') is Staff-scope — de-risking and rebalancing multi-team programs, finding the critical path across teams. (McConnell, Software Estimation: Demystifying the Black Art (Microsoft Press, 2006); Reinertsen, The Principles of Product Development Flow: Second Generation Lean Product Development (Celeritas, 2009); Larson, Staff Engineer: Leadership Beyond the Management Track (self-published, 2021))
- **E5 (= P5)** — Sets decision frameworks for the org. *Evidenced by:* Shapes org-level investment allocation — product vs. platform vs. paydown, honest capacity math, the mechanisms that keep commitments credible — with the economic case written down. *Scope:* organization. *Why this level:* PD-02 P5 ('Sets decision frameworks for the org.') matches shaping org-level investment allocation (product vs. platform vs. paydown) with the economic case written down. (McConnell, Software Estimation: Demystifying the Black Art (Microsoft Press, 2006); Reinertsen, The Principles of Product Development Flow: Second Generation Lean Product Development (Celeritas, 2009); Larson, Staff Engineer: Leadership Beyond the Management Track (self-published, 2021))
- **E6 (= P6)** — Influences company strategic direction through judgment. *Evidenced by:* Answers for the company's biggest technical commitments — to the board, to customers — re-planning publicly when reality changes; their read on feasibility is treated as load-bearing. *Scope:* company. *Why this level:* PD-02 P6 ('Influences company strategic direction through judgment.') is company/industry scope — answering for the company's biggest technical commitments to the board, re-planning publicly when reality changes. (McConnell, Software Estimation: Demystifying the Black Art (Microsoft Press, 2006); Reinertsen, The Principles of Product Development Flow: Second Generation Lean Product Development (Celeritas, 2009); Larson, Staff Engineer: Leadership Beyond the Management Track (self-published, 2021))

##### Strategic & commercial awareness

*Anchor:* Rumelt, Good Strategy Bad Strategy: The Difference and Why It Matters (Crown Business, 2011) — good strategy is diagnosis, guiding policy, and coherent action; engineering choices are strategy in code with a cost model attached, and the habit of connecting work to outcomes starts at E1.

- **E1 (= P1)** — States what business outcome their current task serves — and asks when they can't — treating infrastructure and license costs as real money. *Evidenced by:* Can say, when their tech lead asks, that the ticket they're working means fewer support escalations for a specific customer complaint — and asks what the actual dollar impact is when they genuinely don't know. *Scope:* task. *Why this level:* PD-07 P1 ('States what business outcome their current task serves — and asks when they can't — treating infrastructure and license costs as real money.') is the guided bar by its own text: stating and asking is the Associate register, not yet checking whether it worked. (Rumelt, Good Strategy Bad Strategy: The Difference and Why It Matters (Crown Business, 2011); SFIA Foundation, Skills Framework for the Information Age, version 9 (2024))
- **E2 (= P2)** — Checks whether shipped work actually moved its metric and says so plainly when it didn't; weighs cost and maintenance burden in the technical choices they own. *Evidenced by:* Checks the analytics dashboard two weeks after their checkout-flow change ships, finds conversion unchanged, and says so in the team retro instead of letting the ticket quietly close as 'done'. *Scope:* component. *Why this level:* PD-07 P2 ('Checks whether shipped work actually moved its metric and says so plainly when it didn't; weighs cost and maintenance burden in the technical choices they own.') is the independent-component bar: checking their own shipped work's metric, unaided, is component-scope accountability, one step past merely stating intent. (Rumelt, Good Strategy Bad Strategy: The Difference and Why It Matters (Crown Business, 2011); SFIA Foundation, Skills Framework for the Information Age, version 9 (2024))
- **E3 (= P3)** — Frames a domain's technical decisions in business terms — cost of delay, revenue risk, support load — pushes back on work with weak business rationale, and redirects effort when the numbers say the plan is wrong. *Evidenced by:* Tells the roadmap owner that a requested real-time-sync feature isn't worth the infra spend for the traffic it would see, backs it with the hosting-cost delta and the support-ticket volume it would actually resolve, and gets the plan changed to a cheaper polling approach. *Scope:* capability / domain (terminal level). *Why this level:* PD-07 P3 ('Frames a domain's technical decisions in business terms — cost of delay, revenue risk, support load — pushes back on work with weak business rationale, and redirects effort when the numbers say the plan is wrong.') is the capability-owning bar by its own text: framing 'a domain's' decisions and redirecting effort is domain-scope authority a component owner doesn't have. (Rumelt, Good Strategy Bad Strategy: The Difference and Why It Matters (Crown Business, 2011); SFIA Foundation, Skills Framework for the Information Age, version 9 (2024))
- **E4 (= P4)** — Builds the business case for the cross-team technical investments with the largest return and is trusted by leadership to have done the math. *Evidenced by:* Builds the cost-benefit case for consolidating three teams' duplicate notification services into one shared platform, with the infra-savings and engineering-hours numbers a director signs off on without re-deriving them. *Scope:* multiple teams. *Why this level:* PD-07 P4 ('Builds the business case for the cross-team technical investments with the largest return and is trusted by leadership to have done the math.') is Staff-scope by its own text — cross-team investment cases trusted by leadership are the multi-team register. (Rumelt, Good Strategy Bad Strategy: The Difference and Why It Matters (Crown Business, 2011); Larson, Staff Engineer: Leadership Beyond the Management Track (self-published, 2021))
- **E5 (= P5)** — Aligns an organization's technical strategy with its economics — cost curves, velocity, risk — owning trade-offs measured in staff-years and reporting them in the business's own terms. *Evidenced by:* Reports the org's infrastructure cost curve against velocity to leadership every quarter, and uses it to kill a popular but uneconomical microservices-per-team pattern that was inflating both cloud spend and cross-team coordination cost. *Scope:* organization. *Why this level:* PD-07 P5 ('Aligns an organization's technical strategy with its economics — cost curves, velocity, risk — owning trade-offs measured in staff-years and reporting them in the business's own terms.') is the org-authority bar: aligning 'an organization's' strategy, reported in staff-years, is Senior Staff's org-wide economic accountability. (Rumelt, Good Strategy Bad Strategy: The Difference and Why It Matters (Crown Business, 2011); Larson, Staff Engineer: Leadership Beyond the Management Track (self-published, 2021))
- **E6 (= P6)** — Advises company leadership on where technology changes the business model and is accountable for company-level technology bets paying off. *Evidenced by:* Tells the executive team which of three competing platform bets actually changes the unit economics of the core product, and is the person whose read on that question the CEO uses to decide. *Scope:* company. *Why this level:* PD-07 P6 ('Advises company leadership on where technology changes the business model and is accountable for company-level technology bets paying off.') is company/industry scope — advising leadership on business-model-level bets is the P6 executive-peer register. (Rumelt, Good Strategy Bad Strategy: The Difference and Why It Matters (Crown Business, 2011); Larson, Staff Engineer: Leadership Beyond the Management Track (self-published, 2021))
